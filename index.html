The Battle of Britain

July to October 1940: the first major campaign fought almost entirely in the air.

Presenter name, module, date

Background

Britain alone

By late June 1940 Germany had overrun France, which signed an armistice on 22 June, and British forces had been evacuated from Dunkirk. Britain was the only power in Western Europe still fighting. Hitler expected it to seek terms, and Churchill's government refused.

The German plan

On 16 July Hitler ordered preparations for an invasion, code-named Operation Sea Lion. A crossing of the Channel was not possible while the Royal Air Force could contest the sky above it, so the Luftwaffe was tasked with destroying Fighter Command and winning air superiority over southern England. Two air fleets based in France and the Low Countries carried most of the attack, and a third flew from Norway and Denmark.

The British defence

Air Chief Marshal Sir Hugh Dowding, head of Fighter Command, had built an integrated defence, now called the Dowding System. Chain Home radar stations and the Royal Observer Corps detected raids. Their reports were filtered and plotted at Fighter Command headquarters at Bentley Priory, then passed to Group and Sector control rooms that directed squadrons by radio. Britain did not have to win outright. It had to keep Fighter Command in being.

The forces

The Hurricane and Spitfire defended against Messerschmitt Bf 109 and Bf 110 fighters escorting Heinkel He 111, Dornier Do 17 and Junkers Ju 88 bombers and Ju 87 dive bombers. 11 Group under Air Vice-Marshal Keith Park covered the south-east and London and took the brunt of the fighting. 12 Group under Air Vice-Marshal Trafford Leigh-Mallory covered the Midlands and supported it.

At a glance

Dates
10 July to 31 October 1940, the dates the RAF uses. Some historians start earlier or end later.
Attacker
The Luftwaffe, under Hermann Göring.
Defender
RAF Fighter Command, under Air Chief Marshal Sir Hugh Dowding.
German aim
Air superiority over southern England, as the precondition for invasion.
British aim
Keep Fighter Command in being and deny that air superiority.
Outcome
The Luftwaffe did not gain air superiority, and Operation Sea Lion was postponed.

Key events

Shaded entries are the main turning points.

Channel fighting

10 July to 11 August

  1. 10 July

    Attacks on Channel convoys

    The Luftwaffe steps up attacks on shipping and ports in the Channel. The RAF counts this as the start of the battle.

  2. 16 July

    Directive No. 16

    Hitler orders preparations for the invasion of Britain, Operation Sea Lion.

  3. 1 August

    Directive No. 17

    The Luftwaffe is ordered to defeat the RAF as quickly as possible, ahead of the main offensive.

The assault on Fighter Command

12 August to 6 September

  1. 12 August

    Radar stations and coastal airfields hit

    Raids on the south-coast radar chain and forward airfields open the offensive.

  2. 13 August

    Adlertag, Eagle Day

    The main offensive begins. Poor weather and confused orders make the day's raids uneven.

  3. 15 August

    Heavy fighting along the whole front

    A raid from Norway on north-east England suffers heavy losses, and the Luftwaffe does not repeat a daylight attack from Norway on that scale.

  4. 18 August

    The Hardest Day

    Both sides suffer heavy losses, and Kenley and Biggin Hill are attacked.

  5. 20 August

    Churchill honours the pilots

    In a speech to the House of Commons, Churchill pays tribute to the RAF airmen, later known as "the Few".

  6. 24 August to 6 September

    Sector stations and airfields under sustained attack

    The most dangerous period for Fighter Command. Airfields and sector stations in 11 Group are badly damaged, and losses of pilots and aircraft mount.

  7. Late August to September

    The Big Wing debate

    Park (11 Group) and Leigh-Mallory (12 Group) disagree over whether to meet raids with small, quick interceptions or with large formations assembled first.

  8. 24 to 26 August

    London bombed, then Berlin

    German bombers hit central London by mistake. The RAF raids Berlin the following night, and this is widely seen as helping to provoke the change of German strategy.

London and the turning point

7 to 17 September

  1. 7 September

    Black Saturday

    A massed daylight raid on the London docks. The Luftwaffe shifts its main effort from airfields to London and the Blitz begins, giving Fighter Command room to recover.

  2. 15 September

    Battle of Britain Day

    Two large daylight waves against London are met by the full strength of Fighter Command, and German losses are heavy. The date is commemorated as Battle of Britain Day.

  3. 17 September

    Sea Lion postponed

    Hitler postpones the invasion until further notice.

The battle winds down

October

  1. 12 October

    Invasion put off until spring 1941

    Hitler formally postpones Sea Lion. Daylight raids ebb as the Luftwaffe turns to fighter-bomber sorties and night bombing.

  2. 31 October

    Official end of the battle

    The night Blitz on British cities continues into 1941.
